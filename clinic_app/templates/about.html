The brief we were given

Diabetes is often found late. A confirmatory blood test is cheap, but you have to know who to send. The clinic wanted a way to rank walk-in patients by risk using only questions the front desk already asks — no new equipment, no lab turnaround — and to err on the side of catching people, because telling an at-risk patient they are fine is far worse than sending a healthy one for a cheap test.

How the screening actually works

Everything below was built and measured in a Jupyter notebook, then exported as a single artifact this website loads. The site never retrains anything — it serves the exact model that was evaluated.

1 · The data

The CDC's Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System 2015 — a telephone health survey of 441,456 US adults. After removing "don't know" and "refused" answers and every column that would leak the answer (insulin use, prior diabetes diagnosis, blood sugar readings), 240,184 responses and {{ model_facts.n_features }} features remained.

2 · Nine models, one fair comparison

We trained nine models spanning eight algorithm families — from a single decision tree and k-nearest neighbours through to a support vector machine, a neural network, gradient boosting and a four-family stacking ensemble. Every one goes through the same code path, so they get identical preprocessing and identical validation. Comparing them at the same score would have been misleading, so we instead held recall fixed and asked which model buys that recall most cheaply.

3 · Choosing one, by a rule fixed in advance

The deployed model is {{ model_facts.name }}. It was not picked by preference: a rule written before the results were read selects it — trained on the full dataset, best precision at matched recall, and among models whose accuracy is statistically tied, the one cheapest to ship and run. The last clause matters more than it sounds: an earlier run chose a model that was two orders of magnitude larger for an accuracy difference too small to measure.

4 · Tuned to catch people, not to look accurate

About 16% of people in the data are at risk, so a model that called everyone "fine" would be 84% accurate and clinically worthless. We tune the decision point for recall instead — currently referring at {{ model_facts.threshold_pct }}% estimated risk, which catches roughly 8 in 10 at-risk patients. The deliberate trade-off is more false alarms: most referred patients turn out fine, and a cheap blood test is the right price for that.

5 · The percentage means what it says

A model can rank patients correctly while its numbers are badly wrong — ours read "85%" for a group whose true rate was about 50%. Since we show a percentage to a human, we calibrated it: out of every 100 patients shown "30%", close to 30 really are at risk. Calibration changed no referral decision, only the honesty of the number.

6 · Checked for fairness

A screen that works well on average can still fail a specific group. We audited recall across race, sex, income and age band and report the gap between the best- and worst-served group. Race is predictive but socially sensitive; the notebook argues it should be monitored in production and considered for removal, rather than quietly encoding structural inequity as clinical risk.

What this cannot do

Stating limits plainly is part of the work, not a disclaimer bolted on afterwards.

  • • It cannot diagnose. It estimates risk from self-reported answers. Only a blood test can diagnose diabetes, and a low score is not a clean bill of health.
  • • Most referrals will be false alarms. That is the intended design. At this operating point roughly one in three referred patients turns out to be at risk — the price of missing as few people as possible.
  • • There is a ceiling in the questionnaire itself. Every one of the nine models landed at almost the same accuracy. That is a limit of what survey answers can reveal about blood glucose — not something a fancier algorithm fixes. Better data (a glucose reading, family history) would help; a better classifier would not.
  • • The training data is American. BRFSS surveys US adults, so prevalence and risk-factor patterns may not transfer cleanly to a Singapore population.
  • • This deployment is a simulation. The patients are synthetic and the clinic is not a real medical service. On the hosted demo, records may reset when the server restarts.
